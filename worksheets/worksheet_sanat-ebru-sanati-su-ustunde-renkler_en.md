### 📖 Okuma Parçası (English)
Ebru is a traditional Turkish paper art created on the surface of water. The artist fills a wide shallow tray with a special thickened water.

Natural pigments are sprinkled onto the water using horsehair brushes. The colors spread across the water, forming beautiful floral patterns.

Then clean paper is placed on the water, transferring the artwork onto it. Every ebru is unique; it is impossible to recreate the exact same artwork twice.

### 📖 Okuma Parçası (English)
The quality of ebru art depends upon the chemical balance of natural ingredients. To thicken the water, a natural plant gum called 'kitre' (tragacanth) extracted from astragalus is used.

Ox gall is blended into pigments to prevent them from sinking and help them expand across the surface. The brush handles are invariably fashioned from dried rose twigs due to their optimal elasticity.

### 📖 Okuma Parçası (English)
The foundational and most archaic form of marbling art is known as 'Battal Ebru'. In this style, pigments are scattered freely upon the water bath and transferred to paper without manual intervention.

Subsequently, using slender metallic styluses called 'biz', artists drafted linear waves, creating 'Gelgit' and 'Shawl' motifs. In the 18th century, master Hatip Mehmet Efendi and successors succeeded in rendering concrete floral figures like carnations, tulips, and hyacinths on liquid.

### 📖 Okuma Parçası (English)
Stepping into a traditional ebru studio, one encounters a world markedly distinct from the sterile air of modern art galleries. Here the canvas is neither linen nor wood, but the placid surface of water thickened with natural tragacanth gum. Paints are sourced not from chemical factories but from colorful natural earths, conditioned with ox gall so they float atop the liquid. Flicking pigments with brushes of rose branches and horsehair, the artisan seeks not to subjugate water, but to strike a harmonious dialogue with its fluid grace.

The most enchanting aspect of ebru is that the artwork created on water is irreversibly transferred to paper in a single decisive motion. There is no second attempt. When the sheet is lifted gently from the rim of the trough, the water's surface remains immaculate, while the design is sealed forever into the paper fibers. Hence masters regard ebru not merely as a handicraft, but as visual discipline teaching patience, humility, and the grace of seizing the fleeting moment.
