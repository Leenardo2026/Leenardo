### 📖 Okuma Parçası (English)
In the human brain, sensory inputs like sight, hearing, and touch first pass through a central filter relay called the thalamus. The thalamus evaluates and filters these data, routing them to the cortex only after logical vetting.

However, being evolutionarily our most primal sense, olfaction entirely bypasses the thalamic relay station. Nasal olfactory receptors connect directly with the emotion-governing amygdala and the long-term memory vault in the hippocampus. For this precise reason, when we catch an odor, deep emotional memories ignite at lightning speed before reason can even intervene.

### 📖 Okuma Parçası (English)
French novelist Marcel Proust eloquently recounts how the scent of a madeleine cake dipped in tea teleported him back to his childhood in an instant. That long-forgotten provincial town, the stone mansion, and his aunt's chamber burst back to life in vivid relief with a single fragrant bite. This unforgettable literary episode summarizes a genuine neurological mechanism known in cognitive psychology as the 'Proust Effect': smell is the swiftest key unlocking memory's vaults.

The secret of this extraordinary prowess lies concealed within the anatomical wiring of the human brain. Sensory inputs arriving from our eyes or ears first pass through the thalamus—the brain's rational switchboard—to be filtered and sorted. Yet scent molecules bypass this bureaucratic checkpoint entirely. Scent signals received by nasal receptors plug straight into the amygdala and hippocampus, where emotions and memories are stored. This is why the aroma of damp soil after rain or the whiff of an old wardrobe hurls us back decades—not through logic, but straight through the heart. Photographs show the past; scents resurrect it.
